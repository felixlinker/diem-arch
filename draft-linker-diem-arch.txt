1.  Introduction

   The Digital Emblems (diem) WG works on the digitization of physical
   emblems as _digital emblems_. Emblems convey some information about a
   subject that must be interpreted in the context of some normative
   framework, for example, a law, a treaty, a convention, a government
   agency's procedures, or a customary law.  One famous example of an
   emblem is that of the Red Cross, which conveys that its bearer serves
   a medical purpose and thus enjoys specific protections under
   International Humanitarian Law (IHL).  Digital emblems are systems
   and standards that convey the relevant information by digital means,
   e.g., by storing it in DNS resource records.

   Different use cases have been discussed at the diem WG, for example,
   the labelling of goods in transport by reference to the World Customs
   Organization's classification, conveying that a wetland is designated
   as protected under the Convention on Wetlands of International
   Importance, or the marking of digital services as enjoying specific
   protections under IHL.  (For more details, see
   [I-D.ietf-diem-requirements].)

   Intuition suggests that one design ought to be able to address all
   use cases, and many use cases share some requirements, e.g.,
   authenticating an emblem's information.  Discussions on the various
   use cases, though, have revealed that many use cases have conflicting
   requirements or have different flavours of requirements that may upon
   first glance appear to be the same.  Thus, it seems to be the most
   sensible way forward to distinguish different profiles of Digital
   Emblems, which can address different groups of use cases that share
   relevant requirements.

   In this document, we present these different emblem profiles and
   groups of use cases, outline their domain model and relevant parties,
   and sketch which drafts will comprise a profile.  This document is
   meant to evolve in parallel to the referenced protocol documents.  It
   serves as an overview of the diem WG work.

3.  Digital Emblem Profiles

3.1.  Profile 1: External Registry Emblems

   This profile currently comprises the following drafts:

   *  [I-D.woodcock-faltstrom-external-registry-rrtypes]

   In the external registry profile, emblems convey information that is
   codified by an external registry.  For example, the United Nations
   Economic Commission for Europe (UNECE) defines standardized means to
   encode data, e.g., areas, time and dates, types of cargo, and much
   more.  This profile provides means to associate such externally
   codified information with domain names.

   The domain model of this profile is the following.  A validator
   learns about an asset's associated domain name out-of-band.  Using
   this profile, the validator can query for different groups of
   codified information by querying for relevant resource record types
   associated with that domain name.  How this information is processed
   then depends on the use case and application context.

   Information is published in the DNS by the party controlling the
   asset with which information is associated.  Integrity of the data
   can be provided straightforwardly by signing the relevant zones using
   DNSSEC [RFC4033] [RFC4034] [RFC4035].

3.2.  Profile 2: Protective Emblems

   This profile currently comprises the following drafts:

   *  [I-D.linker-diem-adem-core-02]

   *  [I-D.linker-diem-adem-dns-02]

   The protective emblems use case provides digital means to convey the
   protective emblems defined in the Geneva Conventions, their
   Additional Protocols, and the 1954 Hague Convention.  These emblems
   include the Red Cross, Red Crystal, and Red Crescent for medical,
   humanitarian infrastructure.

   In this profile, an emblem issuer can signal to a validator that the
   services associated with a domain name enjoy the specific protections
   under International Humanitarian Law (IHL), which, for example,
   implies that they should not be disrupted.  To use the emblem on a
   domain name, this domain name must only be used for purposes that
   enjoy the specific protections under IHL.  We assume that the emblem
   issuer controls the domain name, though they may have outsourced its
   operation to a service provider.

   The audience of such emblems consists of validators: all parties who
   would generally disrupt digital services but respect services marked
   with an emblem.  We can thus assume that validators obtain a
   service's domain name out-of-band during the reconnaissance phase of
   their operations.

   The use of the emblem requires authorization by a competent
   authority, typically by a state.  How authorization is obtained is
   outside the scope of this profile for now.
